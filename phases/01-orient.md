# Phase 1 — Orient

## Purpose

Build a useful model of a research field before choosing what to investigate.

Orientation has two distinct modes:

1. **Field cartography** — broad, AI-heavy synthesis of the field's causal and intellectual structure.
2. **Selective evidence inspection** — deep examination of pivotal studies or arguments after the map shows where depth is useful.

Do not default to reading papers sequentially.

---

## Mode A — Field cartography

### Goal

Answer:

- Why does this field exist?
- What original problem motivated it?
- What bottlenecks or assumptions structure the problem?
- What major answers were proposed?
- What failures or evidence generated later branches?
- Which historical questions were answered, weakened, reframed, abandoned, or reopened?
- What are the main current frontier questions?
- How are researchers currently trying to answer them?
- Where is the evidence strong, weak, contradictory, or mostly proxy-based?
- Which parts of the map are uncertain or contested?

### AI should

Perform broad literature work aggressively and in parallel when practical.

Search:
- foundational work;
- major turning points;
- recent work;
- important critiques;
- competing approaches;
- relevant research agendas.

Organize primarily by **questions, bottlenecks, assumptions, and failures**, not by a list of papers.

Prefer a structure like:

motivating problem
→ initial assumptions
→ proposed approach
→ failure/evidence
→ new question
→ competing branches
→ current frontier

Representative papers should support nodes in the map.

Do not make the human consume the search process serially.

### Current-frontier discipline

For each major question or branch, distinguish where possible:

- historically open;
- subsequently investigated;
- substantially answered;
- weakened/reframed;
- technically unresolved but less central;
- currently active;
- newly reopened.

Before highlighting an apparent gap from an older paper, check later literature.

### Output

Present a compact, low-density map.

Breadth is allowed. High-density teaching is not.

A field map may synthesize many papers while still representing one learning objective.

Do not turn every node into a mini-lecture.

### Human should

Use the map to identify:

- problems that genuinely interest them;
- assumptions they want to understand;
- tensions that seem important;
- branches worth deeper inspection;
- research styles they may want to try.

Interest is exploratory, not a research decision.

---

## Mode B — Selective evidence inspection

### Goal

Inspect the pivotal evidence underlying a branch that became interesting during cartography.

Use depth only where it helps the human form an independent view.

### AI should

Choose a small number of representative or decisive studies.

For each, explain:

1. what the researchers wanted to know;
2. what they actually did;
3. what they observed;
4. the relevant baseline or strongest meaningful competitor;
5. what the evidence directly supports;
6. the authors' interpretation;
7. limitations and alternative explanations;
8. what uncertainty remained afterward.

Prefer primary sources.

Verify important quantitative claims carefully.

Do not imply that numbers across papers are comparable unless they actually are.

After a pivotal evidence packet, pause for the human's reaction.

### Human should

Inspect enough evidence to develop reactions such as:

- “I don't understand why this result occurred.”
- “This assumption seems weak.”
- “These two findings seem in tension.”
- “This result does not appear to establish the stronger claim.”
- “This unresolved issue seems important.”
- “I want to understand this branch more deeply.”

The human does not need to formulate a research question yet.

---

## Cumulative field model

As orientation progresses, maintain a compressed model containing:

- motivating problems;
- major bottlenecks;
- important assumptions;
- major approaches;
- established observations;
- known failure modes;
- active open questions;
- historical questions that are now answered or less central;
- contradictory or non-comparable evidence;
- uncertainty about the map itself.

New papers should update this model rather than merely add notes.

---

## Navigation rule

Navigate primarily by **question adjacency**, not paper adjacency.

After a result, ask internally:

> What important question or failure did this expose?

Then:

> What families of work tried to answer it?

Do not automatically ask:

> What paper comes next?

---

## Avoid

- paper-by-paper random walks through citation space;
- equating a missing condition in one paper with a current research gap;
- moving into hypothesis/experiment design during orientation without explicitly deciding to do so;
- making historical future-work statements look like current open problems;
- comparing percentages from incompatible tasks as though they form a leaderboard;
- forcing narrow evidence packets when the actual need is broad cartography;
- turning exploratory interest into a selected direction.

---

## Exit condition

Phase 1 is ready to end only when the human has:

- a useful map of the relevant field or subfield;
- enough understanding of the current frontier to know what questions are genuinely live;
- inspected pivotal evidence in the branches that matter to them;
- identified one or more uncertainties they genuinely want to reason about;
- and explicitly wants to move toward research-question formation.

Merely naming interesting areas is not sufficient.
